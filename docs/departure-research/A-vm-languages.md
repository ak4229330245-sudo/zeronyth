# Topic A — Languages and Runtimes for On-Chain Programs: A Survey

*ZERONYTH departure research. Scope: approaches only, assessed on their own production record. Sources are primary where possible (specs, official docs, repos, papers); URLs are inline next to the claims they support.*

## (a) EVM bytecode VMs and EVM-compatible chains

**Design.** The EVM is a stack machine with 256-bit words, volatile memory, persistent key-value storage, and a program counter; its full state is the tuple `(block_state, transaction, message, code, memory, stack, pc, gas)` https://ethereum.org/whitepaper/ . Every opcode has a gas cost — e.g. `ADD` 3, `SLOAD`/`SSTORE` priced via separate schedules https://ethereum.org/nb/developers/docs/evm/opcodes/ — making execution quasi-Turing-complete: it halts when gas runs out. Code and storage live in the same account; calls are synchronous, dynamically dispatched (`CALL`/`DELEGATECALL`), and composability — especially DeFi composability — is a direct consequence.

**Modern implementations.** Performance no longer comes from the spec but from interpreter engineering. evmone (C++, Ipsilon/Ethereum Foundation) is a standalone EVM exposing the EVMC API, with a baseline interpreter and an "advanced" interpreter using indirect call threading, precomputed per-block gas and stack requirements, and extensive pre-execution bytecode analysis https://github.com/wangkui0508/evmone/blob/HEAD/README.md . revm (Rust) is "focused on speed and simplicity", passes all `ethereum/tests`, and has become infrastructure: it is integrated into Reth, multiple L2s and tooling/block builders, and serves "as a standard for zkVMs" https://docs.rs/crate/revm-context/4.0.0/source/README.md https://bluealloy.github.io/revm/docs/revm/index.html .

**EOF — a cautionary evolution.** The EVM Object Format (EOFv1) proposed a structured container (magic `0xEF00`, typed code/data sections, deploy-time validation, `CALLF` functions, no dynamic jumps) to replace unstructured legacy bytecode https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ipsilon/eof/main/spec/eof.md . After years of work it was removed from the Fusaka/Osaka upgrade in April 2025, citing complexity, timeline risk and lack of rough consensus; Solidity later removed its experimental EOF backend as "obsolete since EOF was rejected for inclusion in the Fusaka network upgrade" https://www.soliditylang.org/blog/category/releases/ , and evmone 0.18.0 (2025-10-31) records "Bye-bye, EOF" — the EOFv1 implementation "has been completely removed" https://github.com/oi2996814/evmone/blob/HEAD/CHANGELOG.md .

**Trade-offs / achievements.** Unmatched network effect, tooling (Solidity/Vyper, Foundry, auditors), and DeFi liquidity proven over a decade. Costs: sequential execution by default; 256-bit arithmetic wasteful for most logic; dynamic dispatch enables reentrancy; unstructured bytecode makes static analysis, formal verification and parallel scheduling hard — precisely what EOF tried, and politically failed, to fix. Parallel EVMs and zkVMs exist, but they work *around* the bytecode model, not because of it.

## (b) Wasm-based contract systems

**Common design.** Contracts are written in mainstream languages (mainly Rust, also C/C++, AssemblyScript, Go) and compiled to WebAssembly, then executed in a sandboxed, metered runtime (Wasmtime/Wasmer/custom). Wasm gives structured control flow, typed locals, a formal spec and near-native speed via JIT/AOT. The catch is universal: *stock Wasm is not consensus-deterministic.* The core spec permits nondeterministic NaN bit-patterns and environment-dependent resource exhaustion, so blockchain runtimes "carve out a deterministic subset" — canonicalising NaNs, disabling threads/relaxed SIMD, and injecting gas metering per basic block https://dev.cube.exchange/what-is/deterministic-execution .

**CosmWasm (Cosmos).** A plug-in module (`x/wasm`) for Cosmos SDK chains; contracts are Rust actors. They cannot call each other directly: a contract finishes, returns messages, and the dispatcher executes them sequentially — "no nested calls are possible. This is essential to avoid reentrancy" — with only a read-only `Querier` for synchronous reads https://raw.githubusercontent.com/filipeom/cosmosSEVM/main/SEMANTICS.md . Upgrades are explicit via an admin `migrate` entry point, not proxies. Production across Osmosis, Neutron, Injective etc.; lesson: actor isolation buys safety, but pushes complexity into message/reply sequencing and atomicity handling, and gas mispricing advisories recur.

**NEAR.** Wasm from the start, one contract per account, sharded execution, asynchronous cross-contract calls as receipts. Gas is calibrated to wall-clock: `1 Tgas ≈ 1 ms`, a chunk cap of `1000 Tgas (~1000 ms)` bounds block time, and gas is prepaid with refunds https://docs.near.org/protocol/transactions/gas https://near.github.io/nearcore/architecture/gas/index.html . It uses a deterministic fork of Wasmer (`near-vm`). Storage staking (locking tokens per byte stored) is a durable anti-bloat lesson. Lesson: async receipts simplify sharding but break synchronous composability that DeFi relies on — a flash loan across contracts is not natural in this model.

**Stellar Soroban.** Wasm (Rust SDK, `wasmi`) deliberately *not* Turing-maximalist: a host/guest split where most work happens in metered host functions/objects. Metering must price host and guest uniformly "in terms of CPU instructions and memory bytes" with pre-calibrated deterministic models, because a contract calling expensive host functions must cost more than one doing pure arithmetic https://developers.stellar.org/docs/learn/fundamentals/fees-resource-limits-metering . Transactions declare a ledger footprint (read/write set) upfront, enabling fee estimation and potential parallelism, plus state archival/rent via TTLs. Mainnet since 2024. Lesson: explicit footprints + host-object model give predictable fees, at the cost of a constrained, platform-specific programming surface.

**Arbitrum Stylus.** A second, co-equal Wasm VM *alongside* the EVM, sharing the same state tree and Solidity ABI: contracts in Rust/C/C++ compile to Wasm (not EVM bytecode), are distinguished by an EOF-inspired `0xEFF00000` prefix, and are activated via the `ArbWasm` precompile https://docs.arbitrum.io/stylus/how-tos/optimizing-binaries . Stylus is additive — EVM contracts are untouched and fully interoperable with Wasm contracts https://blog.arbitrum.foundation/how-wasm-elevates-performance-without-leaving-the-evm/ . Production on Arbitrum One since Sept 2024, with large compute/memory savings for heavy workloads. Lesson: Wasm's value can be captured without abandoning an existing account/ABI model — but ZERONYTH, which is leaving anyway, does not need this compromise.

**EOS/Antelope.** The earliest large-scale Wasm chain (2018, C++ contracts, EOS VM). Notable for making deterministic floating point a runtime guarantee (SoftFloat) because system contracts used `double` math in consensus — i.e. determinism had to be enforced *below* the contract layer https://github.com/hilleywyn/snake/blob/HEAD/audits/chains/AUDIT-EOS-RAM-BANCOR.md , and for resource models (RAM/CPU/NET staking) that were economically brittle. Production but ecosystem-declined; lesson: a fast VM does not rescue weak resource economics or governance.

## (c) Move (Aptos / Sui)

**Design.** Move began at Meta/Diem as "an executable bytecode language" whose key feature is custom resource types inspired by linear logic: "a resource can never be copied or implicitly discarded, only moved between program storage locations" https://www.readkong.com/page/move-a-language-with-programmable-resources-9952091 . Structs carry *abilities* (`copy`, `drop`, `store`, `key`); without `copy`/`drop` an asset cannot be duplicated or silently lost, enforced both by the compiler and by an on-chain bytecode verifier on every published module. There is no dynamic dispatch, hence no classic reentrancy. The follow-up formal work proves resource-safety/conservation properties for the bytecode https://arxiv.org/pdf/2004.05106 . The Move Prover adds specification-level formal verification of invariants, used on the frameworks themselves.

**Two divergent production forks.** Aptos (mainnet Oct 2022) keeps account/global-storage Move and adds Block-STM: optimistic parallel execution over a preset block order with multi-versioned data, re-executing on conflict — the paper reports up to ~170k TPS on Aptos benchmarks, ~20× over sequential on low-contention workloads, with no requirement that developers declare access sets https://aptosnetwork-2iegw07k2-aptos-foundation.vercel.app/whitepaper/aptos-whitepaper_en.pdf . Sui (mainnet May 2023) removed global storage for an object model: every asset is an object with ID, owner and version; transactions list input objects upfront, so non-overlapping transactions execute in parallel and owned-object transfers can bypass full consensus ordering, while shared objects (e.g. a DEX pool) still require it https://gh.hhj520.top/harleyLuke/sui/raw/c7eb3f784cb7ea50bae21f4d0cc5b3956e7ab936/doc/paper/sui.pdf .

**Trade-offs.** Demonstrably the strongest asset-safety story in production, real DeFi at scale, and two working answers to parallelism (optimistic vs. declared-object). Limits: small developer pool, a bespoke toolchain to build and maintain, expressiveness friction (shared-object contention recreates serialisation exactly where DeFi is hottest), and safety that stops at the type system — the 2025 Cetus exploit on Sui was a logic/math bug Move could not prevent. Upgradeability differs by fork: Aptos modules were immutable-by-default; Sui enforces compatible-only package upgrades.

## (d) Solana: SVM, sBPF, Sealevel

**Design.** The Solana Virtual Machine is a register-based runtime executing programs compiled (via LLVM, mainly from Rust) to sBPF — Solana's variant of eBPF bytecode, JIT-compiled to native code https://www.alchemy.com/overviews/what-is-the-solana-virtual-machine . Programs are stateless; *everything* else is an account (data, lamports, owner program, executable flag), and only the owner program may write an account. Crucially, "Solana transactions describe all the states a transaction will read or write while executing" https://www.0xversity.com/blockchain/solana-intro/ .

**Sealevel.** Because read/write sets are declared upfront, the runtime schedules non-overlapping transactions across cores: different accounts → parallel; shared reads → parallel; shared writes → sequential, enforced by account locking https://www.alchemy.com/overviews/what-is-the-solana-virtual-machine . Metering is by compute units/compute budget, not per-opcode gas tables.

**Trade-offs / achievements.** The throughput benchmark for general-purpose chains for half a decade, a single-client-performance-first design now being re-implemented (Firedancer) and exported (SVM rollups/Eclipse). Costs are paid by developers: manually passing every account into every call, rent/account-size friction, contention collapse on hot accounts (popular NFT mints, DEX pools), weak high-level safety (Rust + Anchor conventions, not VM-enforced asset semantics — account-validation bugs are the dominant exploit class), and a historically outage-prone, hardware-heavy validator profile.

## (e) Other notable approaches

**Cairo / Starknet.** Cairo is a language/VM designed so execution traces are efficiently STARK-provable. Modern Cairo compiles to Sierra, a linear-typed intermediate representation that "ensures that programs are always provable by preventing the compilation of programs with infinite loops" and tracks gas, so even failed transactions are provable and chargeable https://docs.starknet.io/build/starknet-by-example/advanced/sierra-ir ; Sierra then compiles to "safe CASM" executed and proven by the Starknet OS http://starknet.io/cairo-book/appendix-09-sierra.html . Native account abstraction (every account is a contract). Production validity rollup; irrelevant proving costs only if ZERONYTH later wants validity proofs — but Sierra's *fail-safe IR* idea (no unprovable/unbounded programs reach the VM) is portable.

**FuelVM / Sway.** Register-based VM over a UTXO model: transactions explicitly list contract UTXOs they touch, so the VM "is able to tell which contracts a transaction will touch before executing any code" and can parallelise execution *and* validation; Sway is a Rust-like DSL with contracts, ephemeral scripts, and stateless *predicates* (pure boolean spend conditions, pruned when spent, no state bloat) https://hackmd.io/@l1-programmability/S11fmSCSs . Fuel Ignition is live as an Ethereum rollup, with modest adoption. Lesson: UTXO + strict access lists give the cleanest parallelism story, at the price of an unfamiliar state model for shared-state DeFi.

**Cadence / Flow.** A high-level resource-oriented language (interpreter/VM, not a systems-language compile target): resources "can only exist in exactly one location and cannot be copied", must be explicitly moved with `<-`, and cannot fall out of scope https://onflow.github.io/docs/cadence/tutorial/resources , with capability-based access control. Production since 2020 (NBA Top Shot scale), Cadence 1.0 shipped 2024 https://deepwiki.com/onflow/cadence . Lesson: resource semantics work at the *language* level too, with better readability, traded against raw performance and hiring pool.

*Honourable mention:* Clarity (Stacks) — decidable, non-Turing-complete, interpreted source on-chain — shows the extreme safety/simplicity end of the spectrum.

## Comparative synthesis: what actually matters for a from-scratch chain

1. **State/access model first, VM second.** Parallelism is decided before any opcode runs: Solana/Fuel/Sui get it from *declared* access sets (accounts, UTXOs, objects); Aptos recovers it optimistically (Block-STM) at re-execution cost; EVM/CosmWasm largely forgo it. For DeFi, model shared state (pools) explicitly — every design serialises there anyway.
2. **Determinism is a subsetting discipline, not a format property.** EVM integers are trivially deterministic; Wasm, eBPF and native compilation all require canonicalisation (NaNs/floats), feature bans, pinned compilers/JITs and version-locked execution. Budget engineering for this from day one.
3. **Metering must price the real resource.** Per-opcode gas (EVM), per-basic-block instrumentation (Wasm), compute units (Solana), host+guest unified models (Soroban) and storage rent/staking (NEAR, Soroban TTL, Solana rent) are all attempts at the same thing: CPU, memory, I/O and *state growth* priced separately and deterministically.
4. **Asset semantics in the type system is the biggest safety lever available.** Move and Cadence eliminate duplication/loss/reentrancy classes *by construction*; EVM, Wasm/Rust and Solana leave them to audits and developer discipline. For a DeFi-core chain this is arguably decisive.
5. **Composability model is a product decision.** Synchronous calls (EVM, Move, Solana CPI) enable atomic multi-protocol DeFi (flash loans, aggregators); actor/async models (CosmWasm, NEAR) are safer and shard-friendly but fragment atomicity.
6. **Verification follows structure.** Structured bytecode + a verifying loader (Move), a provable IR (Sierra), or a decidable language (Clarity) enable prover/static-analysis tooling; unstructured jump-based bytecode (legacy EVM) is why EOF was attempted — and its rejection shows governance/complexity cost is a real constraint even for incumbents.
7. **Upgradeability must be designed, not proxied.** Explicit migration (CosmWasm), compatibility-checked upgrades (Sui), immutable modules (Aptos), upgradeable program buffers (Solana) all beat delegatecall proxies in auditability — pick the governance rule with the VM.
8. **Tooling surface is the adoption cost.** Reusing Wasm or LLVM/eBPF inherits compilers, debuggers and Rust/C ecosystems; a bespoke language (Move, Cairo, Sway, Cadence) buys semantics but means funding the compiler, SDKs, explorer, indexer and auditor ecosystem yourself. Stylus's lesson cuts the other way: reuse a standard bytecode and ABI wherever semantics allow.

Net: the credible from-scratch shortlist clusters into (i) Wasm + a safe systems language + declared footprints, (ii) a resource-typed language/VM in the Move mould, or (iii) an eBPF/register VM with explicit accounts. They differ less in raw speed than in where they place trust: runtime checks, type system, or developer.
